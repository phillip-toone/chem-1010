## 1. Purpose of This Document

This document explains how CHEM 1010 is designed to work as an
instructional system.

It is **not** the semester calendar, syllabus, or a replacement for
Canvas. Those sources answer questions such as *what is assigned*, *when
students should begin it*, and *what policies apply*. This guide answers
a different set of questions:

-   What is the educational purpose of the course?
-   How do the different course components fit together?
-   What is each recurring activity intended to accomplish?
-   What role should the face-to-face instructor play?
-   How should an instructor prepare for a Tuesday/Thursday class
    meeting?
-   How should an LLM help the instructor without inventing course
    requirements?
-   Which aspects of this guide are documented, inferred, or matters of
    instructor practice?

The goal is to make the course understandable as a whole so that an
instructor does not have to reverse-engineer the course one Canvas item
or one lecture at a time.

## 2. Evidence Labels

This guide uses three labels.

**DOCUMENTED**\
Explicitly stated in the syllabus, Canvas calendar, or another
authoritative course artifact.

**INFERRED**\
Strongly suggested by the organization and sequencing of course
materials but not explicitly stated as course policy or philosophy.

**INSTRUCTOR PRACTICE**\
A teaching decision adopted by the instructor. It may be pedagogically
useful without being a formal requirement of the inherited course
design.

These distinctions matter. An inference made while reconstructing the
course should not silently become an official course requirement merely
because it appears in this guide.

## 3. Course Identity

### 3.1 What CHEM 1010 Is

**DOCUMENTED:** CHEM 1010 is an introductory Physical Science General
Education course with no prerequisites. It uses case studies to
demonstrate how chemistry can support individual and community health.
Students are expected to develop fundamental chemistry knowledge,
quantitative problem-solving skills, a modern scientific perspective,
and evidence-based learning strategies.

The course therefore has at least three intertwined purposes:

1.  **Chemistry knowledge and quantitative reasoning**
2.  **Scientific application to meaningful real-world contexts**
3.  **Development of effective, evidence-based learning habits**

The course is not designed merely as a survey of chemistry facts.

### 3.2 Intended Student Population

**DOCUMENTED:** No prior science experience is required.

**INFERRED:** Instruction should therefore avoid assuming that students
already possess a conventional high-school chemistry vocabulary or
mature quantitative problem-solving habits. Foundational ideas and
problem-solving procedures should be made explicit.

### 3.3 General Education Character

**DOCUMENTED:** The course fulfills a Physical Science General Education
requirement. General Education is intended not only to teach
disciplinary knowledge, but also to develop transferable workplace
skills, lifelong-learning strategies, broader perspectives, and the
ability to use information meaningfully.

**INFERRED:** Chemistry content should be taught as a way of reasoning
about the physical world, not solely as material to memorize for an
examination.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. Course Learning Outcomes

**DOCUMENTED:** By the end of the course, students are expected to be
able to:

-   Apply fundamental chemistry concepts---including measurement, atomic
    theory, nomenclature, bonding, molecular geometry, and
    intermolecular forces---to real-world problems from a scientific
    perspective.
-   Demonstrate substantive chemistry knowledge through practical and
    quantitative problem solving.
-   Use the periodic table to characterize and predict properties of
    elements and compounds.
-   Construct three-dimensional models of simple molecules and use
    intermolecular-force concepts to predict physical characteristics.
-   Classify chemical reactions and accurately balance chemical
    equations.
-   Perform multi-step unit conversions and stoichiometric,
    concentration, and empirical-formula calculations.
-   Organize, present, and explain chemistry concepts in writing and
    group settings.

### Teaching implication

**INFERRED:** A successful lecture should normally move students toward
something they can **do, explain, predict, construct, classify,
calculate, or justify**, rather than merely exposing them to
terminology.

## 5. Core Instructional Philosophy

### 5.1 Productive Struggle

**DOCUMENTED:** The course explicitly challenges students to adopt
evidence-based learning strategies. Deliberate Practice is intended to
move students from passive familiarity toward **verifiable mastery
through productive struggle**.

Students should therefore encounter tasks they cannot immediately
complete from recognition alone.

**INFERRED:** The instructor should resist solving every problem for
students before they have attempted it. Worked examples are valuable,
but should be paired with opportunities for students to retrieve,
attempt, diagnose, and correct their own reasoning.

### 5.2 Retrieval Before Re-Exposure

**DOCUMENTED:** Recall, Reflect, and Verify activities require students
to work recall items by hand and from memory, reflect on what they knew,
and verify their work with peers.

**INFERRED:** Retrieval is a recurring design principle. When practical,
students should first try to recall or generate an answer before
immediately consulting notes, slides, solutions, or AI.

### 5.3 Metacognition

**DOCUMENTED:** Unit 0 explicitly addresses mindset, metacognition,
effective strategies, habits, productive struggle, and planning a weekly
study schedule.

**INFERRED:** Teaching students *how to judge what they know* is part of
the chemistry course rather than an optional study-skills add-on.

### 5.4 Consolidation

**DOCUMENTED:** Consolidation Practice uses a directed study guide that
students annotate by hand from memory, encoding concepts and identifying
and filling gaps.

**INFERRED:** Consolidation is intended to organize a section into a
coherent mental model after students have encountered its component
ideas.

### 5.5 Deliberate Practice

**DOCUMENTED:** Deliberate Practice asks students to select a Learning
Objective they have not mastered, generate or obtain a focused problem
set, solve it by hand, verify the solutions, and reflect.

**INFERRED:** Deliberate Practice is not simply "more homework." Its
purpose is targeted correction of a known weakness.

### 5.6 Inquiry and Case Studies

**DOCUMENTED:** The course description identifies case studies as a
central mechanism for demonstrating how chemistry supports individual
and community health. Students are also expected to spend substantial
weekly time in inquiry-based Critical Chemistry lessons.

**INFERRED:** Case studies and Critical Chemistry should be treated as
core learning experiences rather than optional enrichment surrounding
the "real" lecture.

### 5.7 Consistency Over Cramming

**DOCUMENTED:** Students are expected to adopt a consistent weekly study
schedule. The course assigns substantial weekly work across pre-reading,
Critical Chemistry, quizzes, practice, discussion, and exam preparation.

**INFERRED:** The pacing system is deliberately designed to distribute
learning across the week and semester.

## 6. The CHEM 1010 Learning Cycle

The recurring course architecture can be represented as:

``` text
PREPARE
   ↓
ENCOUNTER / EXPLORE
   ↓
APPLY
   ↓
CONSOLIDATE
   ↓
DELIBERATELY PRACTICE
   ↓
ASSESS
   ↓
REFLECT / VERIFY
   ↺
```

This is a working model rather than an official diagram from the
syllabus.

### PREPARE

Students encounter key learning outcomes and introductory resources
through pre-reading quizzes, videos, slides, guided readings, and
related resources.

### ENCOUNTER / EXPLORE

Students develop the concepts through lecture, Critical Chemistry
inquiry, case studies, guided reading, demonstrations, models, or other
learning experiences.

### APPLY

Students work with the concepts through case studies, problem sets,
calculations, models, and in-class activities.

### CONSOLIDATE

Students organize the section into a coherent study structure and
identify gaps.

### DELIBERATELY PRACTICE

Students target a learning objective that remains weak and work
specifically toward mastery.

### ASSESS

Section quizzes, Unit Tests, the Midterm, and the Final provide
progressively broader checks of learning.

### REFLECT / VERIFY

Students retrieve ideas from memory, reflect on what they know, compare
reasoning, and verify understanding.

The exact order can overlap. Learning is iterative rather than perfectly
linear.

**DOCUMENTED FROM UNIT 1:** Recall, Reflect, and Verify should not be
understood only as the final step of this sequence. Retrieval,
reflection, and verification recur **across sections and across time**.
Unit 1 recall sets deliberately mix current material with earlier
Section 1.0/1.1 skills, making spaced retrieval a longitudinal layer
across the learning cycle.

## 7. The "DO Date" Pacing Model

**DOCUMENTED:** Dates on the Canvas Calendar are primarily **Start
Dates**, described to students as **"DO" dates**. They indicate when
students should begin the work rather than the ordinary final deadline.
Unit Tests, Exams, the Signature Assignment, and Reflection use actual
open/close or submission dates.

**DOCUMENTED:** Section assignments remain available through the
corresponding Unit Test close, subject to the course policies described
in the syllabus.

### Instructional meaning

The calendar should be read as a **pacing map**.

A Monday item usually means:

> "Engage with this material now so you are prepared for what follows."

It should not normally be interpreted as:

> "Ignore this until Monday night at 11:59 PM."

**INFERRED:** When planning lectures, the instructor should examine the
DO dates immediately before and after the class meeting. They reveal
what students are expected to have encountered and what they are being
prepared to do next.

## 8. What the Recurring Course Components Are For

### 8.1 Pre-Reading Quizzes

**DOCUMENTED:** Each section contains one or more Pre-Reading Quizzes.
They include Key Learning Outcomes and direct students to video
tutorials, review slides, and other learning resources.

**Working purpose:** Prepare students for deeper engagement by
introducing objectives, vocabulary, representations, and prerequisite
ideas.

**Instructor implication:** A lecture should not necessarily repeat the
pre-reading from beginning to end. Use it as the expected entry point,
while recognizing that students may arrive with uneven preparation.

**DOCUMENTED FROM UNIT 1 / INFERRED PRINCIPLE:** Pre-Reading completion
establishes **exposure, not mastery**. Unit 1 Pre-Reading quizzes often
check recognition of foundational concepts, while later Critical
Chemistry work and Section Quizzes require more complex application.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.2 Guided Readings

**DOCUMENTED:** Guided Readings appear repeatedly in Canvas and, in Unit
1, function as Critical Chemistry lessons with explicit learning
outcomes and completion requirements. Examples include Significant
Figures and the Periodic Table.

**INFERRED:** They provide structured concept development and guided
interpretation rather than serving merely as reading assignments.

**Instructor implication:** Treat Guided Readings as substantive
learning experiences. Determine what students are expected to develop
there before deciding what class time should repeat, clarify, or extend.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.3 Case Studies

**DOCUMENTED:** Case studies are central to the course description and
appear throughout the schedule.

**Working purpose:** Connect chemical concepts to meaningful problems
and contexts, especially those involving individual or community health.

**Instructor implication:** When possible, connect abstract chemistry to
the context students are investigating rather than teaching the
chemistry as an isolated formal system.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.4 Critical Chemistry Lessons

**DOCUMENTED:** Critical Chemistry is required courseware and
constitutes a substantial portion of the course grade. Students are
expected to spend approximately 3--5 hours per week engaged in its
inquiry-based lessons.

**Working purpose:** Provide extended inquiry-based learning outside the
limited face-to-face meeting time.

**Instructor implication:** Do not assume lecture must duplicate the
Critical Chemistry experience. Determine what students are exploring
there and use class time to clarify, connect, model reasoning, diagnose
misconceptions, and extend learning.

**DOCUMENTED FROM UNIT 1:** Critical Chemistry appears in several forms,
including Guided Readings, Case Studies, Problem Sets, and
simulation-linked activities. Canvas often functions as the
orchestration layer: it supplies pacing, outcomes, and a link into the
substantive Critical Chemistry lesson.

**Known unknown:** The page-by-page interactive content of many Critical
Chemistry lessons remains unavailable in this guide.

**DOCUMENTED operational behavior (clarified by the course developer):**
Canvas contains two Critical Chemistry assignment groups with different
purposes:

-   **Critical Chemistry** is created by the Critical Chemistry setup
    and counts for **20% of the course grade**.
-   **Critical Chemistry Assignments** is a **0%** group containing
    ungraded Canvas pointer assignments so the Critical Chemistry
    activities appear in Canvas and on the Canvas calendar.

As students complete assignments within Critical Chemistry, their scores
should populate the graded Critical Chemistry entries in the Canvas
gradebook. A **hyphen** indicates that the student has not completed the
corresponding Critical Chemistry assignment.

**Instructor implication:** Use the graded Critical Chemistry entries to
monitor completion. The course developer also directs instructors to the
CHEM 1010 Teams channel for established strategies for identifying
students who are not completing Critical Chemistry work and messaging
them with Canvas Gradebook tools.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.5 Problem Sets

**DOCUMENTED:** Problem sets occur in several sections.

**INFERRED:** They provide focused quantitative or representational
practice when a topic requires procedural fluency.

**Instructor implication:** Use student difficulty on problem sets to
identify high-value worked examples and misconceptions for class.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.6 Recall, Reflect, and Verify

**DOCUMENTED:** Students use recall practice, work by hand and from
memory, reflect on what they knew, and verify against peers. Discussion
participation includes interaction with classmates.

**Working purpose:** Retrieval practice + metacognitive diagnosis +
social verification.

**Instructor implication:** Treat errors revealed here as useful
evidence about learning, not merely as failures.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.7 Consolidation Practice

**DOCUMENTED:** A directed study guide is generated; students annotate
it by hand from memory, encode concepts, and identify and fill gaps.

**Working purpose:** Build an organized representation of the section
and expose missing connections.

**Instructor implication:** By this stage, instruction should help
students connect ideas rather than merely introduce additional isolated
facts.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.8 Deliberate Practice

**DOCUMENTED:** Students focus on a Learning Objective they have not
mastered, solve a focused set of problems by hand, verify afterward, and
reflect.

**Working purpose:** Convert diagnosed weakness into targeted practice.

**Instructor implication:** Encourage students to choose genuinely weak
objectives rather than comfortable ones.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.9 End-of-Section Quiz

**DOCUMENTED:** A Canvas quiz is assigned at the end of each section.

**Working purpose:** Check section-level learning after preparation,
application, consolidation, and deliberate practice.

**DOCUMENTED FROM UNIT 1:** Actual Section Quiz questions can reveal
assessment scope that is not fully represented by the displayed outcome
list. Section 1.0, for example, assesses scientific notation and
dimensional analysis even though those topics are underrepresented in
its displayed Section Quiz outcomes; Section 1.2 shows much stronger
visible alignment.

**Instructor implication:** When actual assessments are available,
compare them with stated learning outcomes rather than assuming the
displayed outcome list fully defines assessment scope.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.10 Unit Practice Tests

**DOCUMENTED:** Students receive two attempts. Practice Tests are
designed to identify knowledge gaps and reinforce preparation under
conditions similar to the Unit Test. They are not proctored.

**Working purpose:** Diagnostic rehearsal before summative Unit
assessment.

**Instructor implication:** Encourage students to use the first attempt
diagnostically, remediate identified gaps, and then use the second
attempt to test whether those gaps have closed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.11 Unit Tests

**DOCUMENTED:** Four Unit Tests cover multiple sections. They are timed
Canvas tests using Respondus Monitor/LockDown Browser and permit
specified notes/materials.

**Working purpose:** Assess integrated learning across a unit.

**Instructor implication:** Unit review should emphasize relationships
among section objectives and independent problem solving rather than
merely rehearsing quiz questions.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.12 Midterm and Final

**DOCUMENTED:** The Midterm and Final are cumulative, in-person,
proctored Testing Center examinations. Personal notes are not allowed;
specified reference materials are provided.

**Working purpose:** Assess cumulative retention and transfer under more
constrained conditions than Unit Tests.

**Instructor implication:** Students need progressively increasing
retrieval fluency and independence from personal notes as the semester
proceeds.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 8.13 Signature Assignment and Reflection

**DOCUMENTED:** The Signature Assignment and Reflection are General
Education requirements and together form a graded course category.

**Known unknown:** The syllabus excerpt establishes their existence and
weight but does not provide enough detail here to explain their
intellectual purpose or rubric. The assignment prompt and reflection
rubric should be incorporated later.

## 9. Appropriate Role of AI

**DOCUMENTED:** AI use in the AI-Supported Learning Practice is
optional. A complete no-AI path is available.

When AI is used, the syllabus frames it as:

-   **Collaborator during setup**
-   **Auditor during verification**

The student remains responsible for the intellectual work.

### AI should help students:

-   generate recall or practice material;
-   structure a study guide;
-   focus practice on a weak learning objective;
-   check completed work;
-   identify gaps;
-   reflect on discrepancies.

### AI should not replace:

-   retrieval from memory;
-   handwritten problem solving;
-   productive struggle;
-   the student's reasoning;
-   independent performance on assessments.

**DOCUMENTED:** AI and unauthorized aids are prohibited during Unit
Tests; the Midterm and Final also prohibit personal electronic aids
under their testing conditions.

### Instructor principle

AI should increase the quality of practice and feedback **without
removing the cognitive work that produces learning**.

## 10. Role of the Face-to-Face Instructor

This section is partly **INFERRED** because the syllabus specifies
instructor engagement responsibilities but does not prescribe a
minute-by-minute lecture model.

### 10.1 The Instructor Is Not Merely a Content Broadcaster

Students already interact with:

-   pre-reading resources;
-   videos and slides;
-   guided readings;
-   case studies;
-   Critical Chemistry inquiry;
-   practice activities;
-   Canvas quizzes.

Therefore, face-to-face time has higher-value uses than simply
reproducing all asynchronous content.

### 10.2 High-Value Uses of Class Time

**INFERRED / INSTRUCTOR PRACTICE:**

Use class meetings to:

-   establish the conceptual story of the topic;
-   explain difficult ideas and representations;
-   model expert problem-solving decisions;
-   work examples that expose reasoning rather than only algorithms;
-   surface and correct common misconceptions;
-   connect current ideas to prior sections;
-   connect abstract chemistry to case-study contexts;
-   ask students to predict before revealing answers;
-   give students short opportunities to attempt problems;
-   interpret errors;
-   answer substantive questions;
-   show what mastery of the current learning outcomes looks like;
-   prepare students for the next stage of the learning cycle.

### 10.3 A Useful Tuesday/Thursday Rhythm

This is an **INFERRED working model**, not a formal course requirement.

#### Before Tuesday

Students should have begun the first preparation activities associated
with the section.

#### Tuesday

Emphasize:

-   conceptual orientation;
-   prerequisite connections;
-   first major learning objectives;
-   representations and vocabulary;
-   worked examples with reasoning;
-   initial application.

#### Between Tuesday and Thursday

Students continue with the next pre-reading, guided reading, case study,
Critical Chemistry work, or problem set.

#### Thursday

Emphasize:

-   the second major concept where applicable;
-   integration of Tuesday's material;
-   application and problem solving;
-   misconceptions revealed by student work;
-   synthesis of the section;
-   readiness for consolidation, deliberate practice, and the section
    quiz.

This pattern must be adjusted when the calendar indicates exams, review
periods, holidays, or unusually long sections. **Unit 1 evidence
confirms that it should not be forced onto every section:** Section 1.0
spans several quantitative strands across multiple meetings. Once a
section roadmap exists, use its evidence-based lecture rhythm in
preference to this generic pattern.

## 11. How to Prepare a Lecture

Before preparing slides or examples, answer these questions in order:

1.  **Where are we in the course?**\
    Identify Unit, Section, and current learning cycle stage.

2.  **What were students asked to DO before this class?**\
    Inspect the Canvas DO dates and relevant materials.

3.  **What should students already have encountered?**\
    Distinguish exposure from mastery.

4.  **What learning outcomes are active?**\
    Use the Pre-Reading Key Learning Outcomes and course outcomes.

5.  **What comes immediately after this class?**\
    The next case study, problem set, pre-reading, quiz, or assessment
    reveals what today's class needs to prepare students for.

6.  **What prerequisite knowledge does today's topic depend on?**

7.  **What is likely to be difficult or easily confused?**

8.  **What should students be able to do by the end of class that they
    could not reliably do at the beginning?**

9.  **What should students attempt themselves during class?**

10. **How will today's lesson connect backward and forward?**

Only after answering these questions should the instructor decide what
belongs on slides.

## 12. Assessment Architecture

**DOCUMENTED grade categories:**

  Category                                Weight
  ------------------------------------- --------
  Participation                              10%
  AI-Supported Learning Practice             10%
  End-of-Section Canvas Quizzes              10%
  Signature Assignment and Reflection        10%
  Critical Chemistry                         20%
  Unit Tests                                 15%
  Midterm and Final Exams                    25%

### Interpretation

**INFERRED:** The grading structure intentionally distributes credit
across engagement, practice, inquiry, section-level assessment,
unit-level assessment, and cumulative assessment.

No single assessment mode defines success in the course.

The cumulative Midterm and Final together carry the largest listed
category, while substantial credit is also attached to the learning
process itself.

## 13. Course Content Architecture

The semester is organized approximately as follows.

### Unit 0 --- Learning How to Learn Chemistry

Focus: - course orientation; - productive struggle; - mindset and
metacognition; - effective learning strategies; - study habits; -
planning a weekly schedule.

### Unit 1 --- Foundations, Atoms, and Elements

#### Section 1.0 --- Growing Your Math Skills

-   measurement and metric system;
-   scientific notation;
-   significant figures;
-   algebraic equations;
-   dimensional analysis.

#### Section 1.1 --- The Atom

-   atomic structure;
-   subatomic particles and related representations.

#### Section 1.2 --- The Elements

-   periodic table;
-   electron configuration;
-   relationships between atomic/electronic structure and element
    behavior.

### Unit 2 --- Bonding and Molecular Structure

#### Section 2.1 --- Chemical Bonds

-   ionic bonding;
-   covalent bonding;
-   comparison of bond types.

#### Section 2.2 --- Molecular Structure

-   Lewis structures;
-   molecular geometry.

#### Section 2.3 --- Intermolecular Forces

-   intermolecular attractions;
-   relationships among structure, forces, and physical characteristics.

#### Section 2.4 --- Chemical Nomenclature

-   chemical names and formulas.

### Unit 3 --- Aqueous Solutions and Quantitative Reactions

#### Section 3.1 --- Aqueous Solutions

-   aqueous solvation;
-   chemical formula and mole;
-   concentration;
-   units of solution concentration.

#### Section 3.2 --- Chemical Reactions in Aqueous Solutions

-   limiting reactants;
-   quantitative reaction reasoning in solution.

### Unit 4 --- Chemical Reactions and Formulas

#### Section 4.1 --- Chemical Reactions

-   balancing chemical reactions.

#### Section 4.2 --- Classes of Chemical Reactions

-   types and classification of reactions.

#### Section 4.3 --- Chemical Formula

-   empirical formula;
-   molecular formula.

### Cumulative Structure

The conceptual progression is approximately:

``` text
quantitative foundations
        ↓
atomic structure
        ↓
periodicity and electrons
        ↓
chemical bonding
        ↓
molecular structure
        ↓
intermolecular forces
        ↓
nomenclature
        ↓
aqueous solutions
        ↓
moles and concentration
        ↓
quantitative reactions
        ↓
chemical equations and reaction types
        ↓
empirical and molecular formulas
```

**INFERRED:** Later instruction should deliberately reactivate earlier
ideas instead of treating each section as independent.

## 14. Instructor Engagement Responsibilities

**DOCUMENTED:** The syllabus expects instructors to foster a supportive
and interactive environment through substantive engagement.

Responsibilities include:

-   timely responses to student communication;
-   weekly communication of learning outcomes, important dates, and
    course information, either through announcements or face-to-face
    communication;
-   maintaining an accurate Canvas Calendar;
-   participating in course discussions;
-   monitoring student interactions;
-   providing supplemental information and answering questions;
-   proactively contacting students who are not engaging as expected;
-   reconciling the Canvas Gradebook after Unit Tests.

### Teaching implication

The instructor's role includes **maintaining the learning system**, not
merely delivering lectures.

## 15. Student Workload and Course Rhythm

**DOCUMENTED:** Students are told to expect substantial work outside
class, including:

-   pre-reading videos/slides;
-   approximately 3--5 hours per week of inquiry-based Critical
    Chemistry;
-   additional weekly time for Canvas Section Quizzes and Practice
    Tests;
-   additional preparation for cumulative exams;
-   recurring reflective/retrieval work;
-   a consistent weekly study schedule.

### Instructor implication

**INFERRED:** Avoid treating every learning objective as something that
must be fully delivered during lecture. The course intentionally
distributes learning across multiple environments.

At the same time, do not assume completion of an activity guarantees
mastery. Class meetings should help students integrate and make sense of
that distributed learning.

> You are assisting me with teaching CHEM 1010. Treat COURSE-GUIDE.md as

> requirements from inference and instructor choices. Before planning a
> lecture, identify where the class is in the learning cycle, what
> students were expected to do before class, the active learning
> outcomes, and what students need to be prepared to do afterward. Do

### When asked to prepare a lecture, the LLM should:

1.  identify the Unit and Section;
2.  inspect the relevant DO dates;
3.  identify preparation students should already have encountered;
4.  identify active learning outcomes;
5.  determine what comes next;
6.  identify prerequisite concepts;
7.  suggest likely misconceptions;
8.  propose observable end-of-class capabilities;
9.  recommend examples and student practice;

### The LLM should not:

-   invent policies, objectives, or assessment rules;
-   assume a Canvas date is an ordinary deadline when it is a DO date;
-   treat AI-generated answers as a substitute for student productive
    struggle;
-   assume every class meeting should be a conventional content lecture;
-   silently replace the inherited course design with generic
    chemistry-course conventions;
-   present an inference as documented fact.

## 18. Known Unknowns

This first version intentionally leaves several questions unresolved.

### Lecture design

substantive Critical Chemistry learning experiences with explicit

also need clarification of the Critical Chemistry-to-Canvas

demonstrating that displayed outcome lists should be audited against the

section roadmaps should identify the operative mastery outcomes and
explicitly record alignment discrepancies rather than silently
reconciling them.

## 20. Working Summary

CHEM 1010 is best understood not as a sequence of lectures followed by
homework, but as a **distributed learning system**.

Students repeatedly:

**prepare → encounter/explore → apply → consolidate → deliberately
practice → assess → reflect/verify**

The instructor's job is to help that system work:

-   clarify difficult chemistry;
-   connect ideas;
-   model scientific and quantitative reasoning;
-   create opportunities for productive struggle;
-   diagnose misconceptions;
-   help students recognize gaps;
-   connect asynchronous work to face-to-face learning;
-   maintain pacing and communication;
-   prepare students for increasingly independent performance.

The central planning question for a class meeting is therefore not
simply:

> "What chapter am I supposed to lecture on today?"

It is:

> **"Where are students in the learning cycle, what should they already
> have encountered, what chemistry do they need help making sense of
> today, and what must they be ready to do next?"**

That question should guide lecture preparation throughout the course.
