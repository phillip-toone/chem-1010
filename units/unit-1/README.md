## 1. Unit 1 at a Glance

Unit 1 builds two foundations at the same time:

1.  the **quantitative tools** students need to solve chemistry
    problems; and
2.  the **atomic/electronic model** students need to reason about matter
    and the elements.

The unit can be understood as a progression:

``` text
Section 1.0
Growing Your Math Skills
Build the quantitative toolkit
        |
        v
Section 1.1
The Atom

        |
        v
Section 1.2
The Elements

        |
        v
UNIT 1 TEST
        |
        v
Ready to ask:
How do atoms interact with one another?
```

That final question provides a natural conceptual bridge into later work
on chemical bonding.

## 2. Why These Three Sections Belong Together

At first glance, "Growing Your Math Skills," "The Atom," and "The
Elements" can look like three unrelated topics. Taken together, however,
they establish the language students need for the rest of introductory
chemistry.

### Section 1.0 gives students the quantitative language

Students learn to work with:

-   measurements;
-   units;
-   uncertainty;
-   scientific notation;
-   significant figures;
-   algebraic relationships;
-   proportional reasoning;
-   dimensional analysis.

The section establishes the expectation that numbers in chemistry are
not isolated arithmetic values. They carry **units, precision,
relationships, and physical meaning**.

### Section 1.1 gives students a model of what matter is made of

Students develop relationships among:

``` text
protons   -> elemental identity / atomic number
neutrons  -> isotope / mass number
electrons -> ionic charge
```

They then use atomic notation, isotopes, average atomic mass, ions, and
beta decay to apply that model.

### Section 1.2 extends the model to explain the elements

Students connect atomic identity to electronic structure:

``` text
atomic number
    |
    v
element
    |
    v
electron configuration
    |
    v
period / block / valence pattern
    |
    v
periodic behavior
```

The periodic table becomes more than a reference chart: it becomes a map
of recurring electronic and chemical patterns.

## 3. The Unit-Level Learning Story

A useful instructor narrative for Unit 1 is:

### First: learn how chemists handle quantities

Before students can reason quantitatively about chemistry, they need a
dependable way to work with measurement, scale, uncertainty, equations,
and units.

Section 1.0 establishes those habits.

### Next: learn what an atom is

Section 1.1 shifts from quantitative foundations to a particle-level
model of matter.

Students learn that changing different subatomic particles has different
consequences:

-   change protons → change the element;
-   change neutrons → change the isotope;
-   change electrons → change the charge.

### Then: learn why the elements form patterns

Section 1.2 develops the electron portion of the atomic model.

Students move from a simple Bohr representation toward shells,
subshells, orbitals, ground-state electron configurations, and a
probability-based model. They connect these ideas to periodic-table
organization and broad chemical behavior.

### By the end of the unit

Students should possess enough quantitative and atomic/electronic
language to begin studying how atoms combine and interact.

## 4. Documented Unit 1 Learning Outcomes

The "Begin Unit 1" Canvas page lists the following outcomes:

### Quantitative foundations

-   Make use of dimensional analysis to solve problems and give answers
    with appropriate units.
-   Perform math operations and report results to the appropriate number
    of significant figures.
-   Distinguish between an exact number and a measurement with
    uncertainty.
-   Make use of the principles of algebra to solve for the missing
    variable in an equation.
-   Determine whether the quantities in a proportionality have a direct
    or indirect relationship.

### Atomic structure

-   Use atomic symbols to explain how elements differ from one another.
-   Determine the charge of an ion by comparing the number of protons
    and electrons.
-   Differentiate between a cation and an anion.
-   Distinguish between different isotopes of a given element.
-   Demonstrate, using elemental symbols, how beta decay produces new
    elements with the same atomic mass number.

### The elements and electronic structure

-   Identify metals, nonmetals, and metalloids by their properties
    and/or location on the periodic table and predict their properties.
-   Make use of the periodic table to determine the atomic symbol, the
    number of protons, and the average mass of an element.
-   Make use of the Aufbau Principle and the periodic table to determine
    the electron occupancy of atoms in the ground state.
-   Use the Pauli Exclusion Principle and Hund's Rule to construct
    orbital diagrams for ground-state atoms.
-   Use the octet rule to generate stable electron configurations for
    neutral atoms and ions.
-   Differentiate between the static Bohr model and the probability
    model used in modern quantum mechanics.

These unit-level outcomes are useful for seeing what the course
considers important across section boundaries.

## 5. How the Sections Depend on One Another

The sections are not independent modules.

### 1.0 → 1.1

Section 1.0 skills support Section 1.1 work involving:

-   average atomic mass;
-   isotopic abundance;
-   units;
-   calculated quantities;
-   significant figures.

Section 1.1 recall practice deliberately reactivates scientific
notation, significant figures, and dimensional analysis.

### 1.1 → 1.2

Section 1.2 assumes students already understand:

-   elemental identity from proton count;
-   atomic number;
-   electrons;
-   neutral atoms and ions;
-   atomic notation;
-   average atomic mass;
-   the introductory Bohr representation.

Section 1.2 then asks how electrons are arranged and how those
arrangements relate to the periodic table.

### 1.0 → 1.2

Section 1.2 recall practice continues to reactivate quantitative skills
from Section 1.0, demonstrating that the course intends these skills to
remain active rather than disappear after the first section.

## 7. Role of Face-to-Face Instruction Across Unit 1

> **Status: INFERRED / INSTRUCTOR PRACTICE**

The inherited materials provide substantial asynchronous instruction.
Face-to-face class therefore adds the most value when it helps students
**connect, organize, reason, practice, and diagnose** rather than merely
repeating what they encountered elsewhere.

### In Section 1.0

Establish consistent quantitative habits:

-   write units;
-   map conversion pathways;
-   isolate variables;
-   distinguish exact and measured quantities;
-   connect significant figures to uncertainty;
-   use dimensional analysis;
-   check units and reasonableness.

### In Section 1.1

Make the atomic model coherent:

``` text
proton  -> identity
neutron -> isotope
electron -> charge
```

Help students move among particle counts, atomic symbols, verbal
explanations, and calculations.

### In Section 1.2

Connect the two halves of the section:

``` text
periodic-table pattern
        ↕
electronic structure
```

Help students move among element identity, table position, electron
configuration, orbital diagrams, valence structure, and broad chemical
behavior.

## 9. What Students Should Carry Into the Unit 1 Test

Students should not prepare for Unit 1 as three isolated lists of facts.

They should be able to combine skills and representations.

Examples include:

-   perform a unit conversion and report a justified number of
    significant figures;
-   rearrange an equation before substituting values;
-   use units to check whether a setup is sensible;
-   infer element identity from atomic number or proton count;
-   distinguish isotope identity from ionic charge;
-   interpret atomic symbols;
-   connect average atomic mass to isotopic composition;
-   use periodic-table position to classify an element;
-   derive a ground-state electron configuration from the periodic
    table;
-   interpret or construct an orbital diagram;
-   use valence-electron/octet reasoning to explain common ion
    formation;
-   distinguish the Bohr model from the modern probability/orbital
    model.

The exact Unit 1 Test weighting of these abilities remains to be
verified from the test itself.

## 10. What Students Should Carry Forward After Unit 1

Unit 1 is foundational rather than self-contained.

Students should carry forward:

### Quantitative habits

-   units remain visible throughout calculations;
-   significant figures communicate justified precision;
-   equations can be rearranged;
-   dimensional analysis provides a general conversion/problem-solving
    method;
-   results should be checked for reasonable units and magnitude.

### Atomic model

-   proton count defines elemental identity;
-   neutron count distinguishes isotopes;
-   electron count determines ionic charge.

### Electronic model

-   electrons occupy quantized structures described through shells,
    subshells, and orbitals;
-   ground-state occupancy follows working rules such as Aufbau, Pauli,
    and Hund;
-   valence electrons are especially important for chemical behavior;
-   periodic-table structure reflects recurring electronic patterns.

These ideas provide prerequisites for later work on bonding, molecular
structure, nomenclature, reactions, and quantitative chemistry.

## 14. Instructor Planning Questions

When planning or revising Unit 1, ask:

1.  What must students already know before this class meeting?
2.  Which resource is supposed to provide that initial learning?
3.  What conceptual connection can face-to-face instruction make that
    the resources do not make clearly enough?
4.  What should students be able to *produce without help* by the end of
    the meeting?
5.  Which prior skills should be retrieved again rather than allowed to
    fade?
6.  Does the assessment actually measure the stated learning outcomes?
7.  Are students being asked to navigate unnecessary competing
    terminology or organizational systems?
8.  Where are students likely to confuse recognition with mastery?
