## 1. Why This Section Exists

Section 1.1 develops a working model of the atom that students can use
to reason about elemental identity, isotopes, ions, atomic notation,
average atomic mass, and nuclear change.

A useful conceptual center is:

``` text
                         ATOM
                           |
              +------------+------------+
              |            |            |
            proton       neutron      electron
              |            |            |
              v            v            v
           identity      isotope       charge
              |            |            |
        atomic number   mass number     ion
             (Z)           (A)       cation/anion
                           |
                    isotopic abundance
                           |
                   average atomic mass
```

> **Protons determine elemental identity. Neutrons distinguish isotopes.
> Electrons determine ionic charge.**

This is an instructor synthesis of the documented learning outcomes and
activities, not official inherited wording.

## 3. Documented Section Learning Outcomes

Students are expected to:

1.  Describe how Dalton's postulates of Atomic Theory help us understand
    atoms, compounds, and chemical reactions.
2.  Explain the major characteristics of each subatomic particle.
3.  Use the Bohr model to describe the composition and structure of the
    atom.
4.  Write and interpret symbols depicting atomic number, mass number,
    and charge of an atom or ion.
5.  Use atomic symbols to explain how elements differ.
6.  Use atomic symbols to explain how isotopes of an element differ in
    neutron number.
7.  Define the atomic mass unit and average atomic mass in the context
    of isotopes and isotopic abundance.
8.  Define an ion, determine charge from proton/electron counts, explain
    how ions differ from neutral atoms, and distinguish cations from
    anions.
9.  Show, using elemental symbols, how beta decay produces new elements
    with the same atomic mass number.

These outcomes appear in the Pre-Reading and again in Consolidation
Practice, so they should be treated as the primary backbone of Section
1.1.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. What Students Should Be Able to Do

By the end of the section, students should be able to:

-   identify protons, neutrons, and electrons by location, relative
    mass, and charge;
-   explain how Dalton's model connects atoms, compounds, and chemical
    reactions;
-   use a simple atomic model to relate nucleus, protons, neutrons, and
    electrons;
-   determine elemental identity and atomic number from proton count;
-   determine mass number from proton and neutron counts;
-   determine neutron count from mass number and atomic number;
-   determine neutrality or ionic charge from proton/electron counts;
-   distinguish cations and anions;
-   interpret and construct atomic/isotopic symbols;
-   distinguish isotopes of the same element;
-   connect isotopic abundance to average atomic mass;
-   interpret beta decay as nuclear change that can produce a new
    element while mass number remains constant;
-   connect selected radioactive isotopes to medical applications.

Exact assessment emphasis should be checked against the Section Quiz and
Unit Test.

## 7. Role of Each Component

### Recall, Reflect, and Verify

**Purpose:** retrieval practice, metacognition, spaced repetition, and
peer verification.

Students attempt work from memory, reflect on what the attempt revealed,
adjust their study strategy, and compare with a peer. The recall set
deliberately mixes current atomic-structure ideas with prior Section 1.0
skills.

### Atomic Structure Pre-Reading and Quiz

**Format:** 15 questions, 15 points, two attempts, no time limit.

**Purpose:** establish the conceptual territory and provide initial
exposure to vocabulary, models, relationships, and resources. Completion
should not be treated as evidence of mastery.

### Critical Chemistry Case Study --- Atomic Structure

**Purpose:** inquiry/application.

Its documented outcomes span subatomic particles, atomic mass/mass
number, isotopes, ions, radioactive decay, and medical uses. Canvas
mainly launches and records completion; the substantive lesson occurs in
Critical Chemistry.

### Critical Chemistry Atomic Structure Problem Set

**Purpose:** focused operational practice.

It emphasizes particle properties, proton count and identity, ionic
charge, cation/anion distinctions, isotopes, and mass number.

### Mini-PhET Build an Atom

**Purpose:** manipulate a particle-level model.

``` text
change protons   -> change element
change neutrons  -> change isotope
change electrons -> change charge
```

This is a central organizing relationship for the section.

### Consolidation Practice --- The Atom

**Purpose:** build an incremental Unit Test study guide from memory and
expose gaps early.

Students generate a directed guide, audit it against official outcomes,
annotate it by hand, encode concepts, work examples, mark gaps with
`✓ ~ ? ! →`, and reflect. It becomes part of a cumulative Unit Test
guide.

### Deliberate Practice --- The Atom

**Purpose:** targeted practice on a known weakness.

Students choose a difficult outcome, formulate an Understanding
Statement and Essential Question, solve 10 focused problems by hand,
verify afterward, annotate corrections, distinguish mathematical from
conceptual errors, and choose a forward strategy. AI is optional.

### Section Quiz 1.1 --- The Atom

**Purpose:** end-of-section assessment.

The Fall 2026 schedule lists it as 25 points. The actual quiz
blueprint/questions were not provided, so exact outcome alignment
remains unknown.

## 9. Conceptual Story

### A. Why believe in atoms?

Dalton's theory gives a particle-level explanation for observations
about matter, compounds, and chemical change.

### B. What is inside an atom?

Experimental evidence leads to a structured atom containing a small
nucleus and subatomic particles.

### C. What determines elemental identity?

``` text
# protons = atomic number (Z) = elemental identity
```

### D. What can vary without changing the element?

``` text
same proton count + different neutron count = isotopes
mass number (A) = protons + neutrons
```

### E. What determines charge?

``` text
charge = protons - electrons
```

Changing electron count produces ions without changing elemental
identity.

### F. Why is periodic-table atomic mass usually not a whole number?

Naturally occurring samples contain mixtures of isotopes. Isotopic
masses and relative abundances combine to give an average atomic mass.

### G. Can elemental identity change?

Yes. Nuclear change can alter proton count. Beta decay provides a first
example in which elemental identity changes while mass number can remain
constant.

### H. Why does this matter?

Radioactive isotopes have diagnostic and therapeutic medical
applications, connecting atomic structure to the course's
health-oriented emphasis.

## 10. Proposed Role of Face-to-Face Instruction

> **Status: INFERRED / INSTRUCTOR PRACTICE.** The supplied materials
> establish content and activities but do not prescribe a
> minute-by-minute lecture model.

The asynchronous system already provides substantial exposure, inquiry,
simulation, and practice. Class time can add value by helping students:

-   organize the conceptual story;
-   connect experimental evidence to models;
-   reason through relationships rather than memorize isolated
    definitions;
-   move among particle pictures, words, equations, and atomic symbols;
-   expose and correct misconceptions;
-   predict consequences of changing proton, neutron, or electron count;
-   reason through atomic symbols and average atomic mass;
-   connect beta decay to proton count and elemental identity;
-   connect chemistry to medical applications.

## 11. Proposed Tuesday/Thursday Rhythm

### Tuesday --- Build the Atomic Model

**Students should have encountered:** Recall/Reflect/Verify, the
Pre-Reading, and the beginning of the Critical Chemistry Case Study.

**Teaching goals:**

1.  Explain why atomic theory is useful.
2.  Connect Dalton and later evidence to an evolving atomic model.
3.  Establish location, relative mass, and charge of proton, neutron,
    and electron.
4.  Establish nucleus/electron-region structure.
5.  Make proton count the anchor for identity.
6.  Introduce atomic number and mass number.
7.  Begin particle-count ↔ atomic-symbol reasoning.

**Student practice:** Given p, n, and e, determine element, atomic
number, mass number, neutrality/charge, and isotope, while explaining
why.

**End-of-class target:** Students can explain the basic particle model
and use particle counts to determine identity, mass number, and
introductory charge relationships.

### Between Tuesday and Thursday

Students continue/complete the Critical Chemistry Case Study, Atomic
Structure Problem Set, and Mini-PhET Build an Atom.

### Thursday --- Use the Atomic Model

**Teaching goals:**

1.  Consolidate proton → identity, neutron → isotope, electron → charge.
2.  Develop fluency with atomic/isotopic symbols.
3.  Distinguish neutral atoms, cations, and anions.
4.  Develop isotope and average-atomic-mass reasoning.
5.  Connect nuclear change to elemental identity.
6.  Introduce beta decay and medical relevance at the level required by
    the outcomes.
7.  Prepare students to identify remaining gaps through Consolidation
    and Deliberate Practice.

**Student practice:** Mix particle diagrams, atomic symbols, p/n/e
counts, ions, isotopes, abundance data, and simple beta-decay
representations.

**End-of-class target:** Students can use the atomic model flexibly
across particle, symbolic, and simple quantitative representations.

## 12. Common Confusions to Watch For

> **Status: candidates to verify against actual student work.**

-   atomic number vs. mass number;
-   neutron count vs. elemental identity;
-   thinking electron gain/loss changes the element;
-   cation vs. anion;
-   interpreting positive ions as gaining positive particles rather than
    losing electrons;
-   isotope mass number vs. average atomic mass;
-   periodic-table atomic mass vs. mass number of one atom;
-   placement of atomic number, mass number, and charge in symbolic
    notation;
-   assuming all atoms of an element have identical neutron counts;
-   failing to connect beta decay's proton-count change to elemental
    identity;
-   treating the Bohr model as a complete modern description rather than
    a useful model.

## 13. How Section 1.1 Feeds Forward

### Into Section 1.2 --- The Elements

Atomic number anchors elemental identity and periodic-table position.
Electron concepts prepare students for electron configuration. Ions
require proton/electron reasoning. Isotopes and average atomic mass
explain periodic-table information.

### Into Unit 2

Students will need electron concepts, ions and ionic charge, elemental
identity, and symbolic notation for bonding and molecular structure.

### Into Later Quantitative Chemistry

Students must distinguish mass number from average atomic mass and move
between symbolic chemical information and measured quantities.

### Into Chemical Reactions

Dalton and conservation of matter provide conceptual groundwork for
balancing and interpreting chemical reactions.

## 17. Instructor Quick Reference

**Section purpose:** Build a usable particle model of the atom.

**Core relationships:**

``` text
protons   -> element identity / atomic number
neutrons  -> isotope / mass number
electrons -> ionic charge
```

**Tuesday:** Build the model and relationships.

**Thursday:** Use the model across symbols, ions, isotopes, average
atomic mass, and beta decay.

**Organize around CHEM 1010 outcomes, not OpenStax numbering.**

**Do not duplicate Critical Chemistry.** Use class to connect, clarify,
model reasoning, diagnose misconceptions, and prepare students to
practice independently.

**End goal:** Students move among particle counts, atomic symbols,
verbal explanations, and simple calculations while explaining why the
relationships work.
