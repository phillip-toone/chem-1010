# Section 1.2 --- The Elements

## Instructor Teaching Roadmap

> **Course:** CHEM 1010 --- Introductory Chemistry\
> **Unit:** 1\
> **Section:** 1.2 --- The Elements\
> **Status:** Working instructor roadmap

## 1. Why This Section Exists

Section 1.2 connects **where an element appears on the periodic table**
with **how its electrons are arranged** and, in turn, with broad
patterns in chemical behavior.

``` text
atomic number → element identity → electron configuration
                                      ↓
                            period / block / valence pattern
                                      ↓
                             broad chemical behavior
```

> **The periodic table is not merely a list of elements; its structure
> reflects recurring patterns in electronic structure.**

This is an instructor synthesis of the documented outcomes and
assessments.

## 3. Operative Section Learning Outcomes

The identical 10-outcome set used in Consolidation Practice, Deliberate
Practice, and Section Quiz 1.2 provides the strongest evidence for
section-level mastery. Students should:

1.  Describe periodic law and periodic-table organization.
2.  Predict general properties from table location.
3.  Identify metals, nonmetals, and metalloids by properties/location.
4.  Use the table to determine atomic symbol, proton count, and average
    mass.
5.  Match elements to periodic-table groups.
6.  Apply the Aufbau Principle to ground-state occupancy.
7.  Derive predicted ground-state electron configurations using the
    periodic table.
8.  Apply Pauli Exclusion and Hund's Rule to orbital diagrams.
9.  Use the octet rule to explain stable electron configurations.
10. Distinguish the static Bohr model from the probability model of
    modern quantum mechanics.

## 4. Supporting Activity Outcomes

**Periodic Table Pre-Reading:** periodic law, organization, property
prediction, metals/nonmetals/metalloids, element names/symbols.

**Critical Chemistry Guided Reading:** classification by position;
symbol/proton count/average mass; periods/groups; periodic law;
main-group, transition, and inner-transition metals.

**Electron Configuration Pre-Reading:** ground-state configurations;
Pauli/Hund; core versus valence electrons; configuration exceptions;
relationship to periodic classification.

**Critical Chemistry Electron Configuration Case Study:** quantum-number
language; octet rule; Bohr versus probability model; Aufbau;
shells/subshells/orbitals; Hund/Pauli; ground states; Aufbau exceptions.

## 5. What Students Should Be Able to Do

Students should be able to navigate groups and periods; identify common
groups; classify metals/nonmetals/metalloids; extract atomic
information; and use location to make broad property predictions.

They should also distinguish shells, subshells, and orbitals; associate
`s,p,d,f` with subshells; determine orbital/electron capacities; apply
Aufbau, Pauli, and Hund; construct and interpret full configurations,
shorthand configurations, and orbital diagrams; distinguish core and
valence electrons; relate configurations to periodic-table blocks; and
use octet-rule reasoning for common ions.

## 6. Prerequisites

From Section 1.1: proton/electron reasoning, atomic number, elemental
identity, ions, average atomic mass, and the introductory Bohr
representation.

From Section 1.0: scientific notation, significant figures, and
dimensional analysis remain active through spaced recall.

Students also continue retrieval, consolidation, deliberate practice,
reflection, and verification.

## 8. Role of Each Component

### Recall, Reflect, and Verify

Cumulative retrieval spanning Sections 1.0--1.2: periodic patterns,
average atomic mass, dimensional analysis/sig figs, element
classification, electron configuration, valence electrons, octet-rule
ions, and Bohr-versus-modern models.

### Periodic Table Pre-Reading

10 questions, two attempts, no time limit. Introduces periodic law,
organization, classifications, prediction, and names/symbols. Review
points to Part A PDF.

### Critical Chemistry Guided Reading

Structured engagement with table position, atomic information,
periods/groups, periodic law, metal categories, and groups.

### Electron Configuration Pre-Reading

10 questions, two attempts, no time limit. The actual quiz checks `n`,
shells/subshells, energy, low-energy filling, configuration notation,
hydrogen `1s1`, table/configuration relationship, orbital diagrams, and
valence/core electrons. Review points to Part B PDF.

### Critical Chemistry Case Study

Develops configuration using quantum-number framing, octet rule, modern
probability model, Aufbau, shell/subshell/orbital hierarchy, Hund,
Pauli, ground states, and exceptions.

### Consolidation Practice

Retrieval-based cumulative Unit 1 guide audited against all 10 operative
outcomes.

### Deliberate Practice

Targeted practice on a weak outcome. Unlike Section 1.1, all 10
operative outcomes are selectable.

### Section Quiz 1.2

35 questions, two attempts. It assesses both periodic-table reasoning
and electronic structure, including groups, periods, atomic number,
transition metals, shells/subshells/orbitals, Aufbau, blocks, full
configurations, identifying elements from configurations, orbital
diagrams, Hund, Pauli, shorthand notation, octet rule, ion formation,
and isoelectronic ions.

## 9. Source Crosswalk

### Part A --- The Periodic Table

Supports organization by atomic number, groups/periods, similar group
properties, metals/nonmetals/metalloids, element symbols, common groups,
and broad ion tendencies.

### Part B --- Electronic Configuration

Supports discrete energies, Bohr model, shells/subshells/orbitals, spin,
full/shorthand configurations, Aufbau, Pauli, Hund, common isoelectronic
ions, probability/orbital model, and orbital shapes.

## 10. Conceptual Story

**A. The table contains patterns.** Atomic number orders the elements;
rows and columns expose recurring similarities.

**B. Broad regions matter.** Metals, nonmetals, metalloids, main-group,
transition, and inner-transition regions provide predictive information.

**C. The electron model becomes more sophisticated.** The simple Bohr
picture gives way to shells, subshells, orbitals, and probability.

**D. Occupancy follows rules.** Aufbau, Pauli, and Hund provide working
rules for ground-state configurations.

**E. The periodic table is an electron-configuration map.** Its `s`,
`p`, `d`, and `f` blocks reflect subshell filling.

**F. Valence electrons connect configuration to chemistry.** Outer
electrons help explain recurring group behavior.

**G. Stable configurations help explain common ions.** The course uses
octet-rule reasoning to connect metal/nonmetal tendencies to
noble-gas-like configurations.

## 11. Proposed Role of Face-to-Face Instruction

> **INFERRED / INSTRUCTOR PRACTICE**

Class should help students see one coherent system rather than two
unrelated topics. High-value work includes reading the table as
information, connecting group/period/block to electronic structure,
contrasting Bohr and probability models, clarifying
shell→subshell→orbital hierarchy, modeling Aufbau/Hund/Pauli decisions,
deriving configurations from the table, moving among representations,
and connecting valence structure to ion behavior.

## 12. Proposed Tuesday/Thursday Rhythm

### Tuesday --- Read the Periodic Table as a Map

Students should have encountered Recall/Reflect/Verify, Periodic Table
Pre-Reading, and Guided Reading.

Teach periodic law, atomic-number organization, groups versus periods,
metals/nonmetals/metalloids, major regions/groups, and broad property
prediction. End with the question: **why do these patterns repeat?**

**End target:** Students navigate the table confidently and use position
to classify elements and make broad predictions.

### Thursday --- Explain the Map With Electrons

Students should have encountered the Electron Configuration Pre-Reading
and Critical Chemistry Case Study.

Teach Bohr→probability transition, shell/subshell/orbital hierarchy,
`s,p,d,f` capacities, Aufbau/Pauli/Hund, configurations/orbital
diagrams, the table as a configuration map, core/valence electrons,
blocks/groups, and octet-rule/common-ion reasoning.

``` text
element
  ↕
periodic-table position
  ↕
full configuration
  ↕
noble-gas shorthand
  ↕
orbital diagram
  ↕
valence pattern
```

**End target:** Students use the periodic table as a practical map of
ground-state electron structure and connect electronic structure to
broad periodic behavior.

## 13. Common Confusions to Watch For

Candidates to verify from student work:

-   group vs. period;
-   atomic number vs. average atomic mass;
-   metal/nonmetal/metalloid boundaries;
-   shell vs. subshell vs. orbital;
-   orbital vs. electron;
-   Bohr orbit vs. modern probability description;
-   Aufbau vs. Hund vs. Pauli;
-   pairing before singly occupying degenerate orbitals;
-   orbital capacity vs. subshell capacity;
-   `4s`/`3d` ordering;
-   memorizing configurations instead of deriving them;
-   core vs. valence electrons;
-   noble-gas shorthand;
-   overgeneralizing the octet rule;
-   isoelectronic-ion reasoning;
-   thinking an ion literally becomes a noble gas.

## 16. Maintenance Notes

1.  **Principal quantum number:** several items say "principle quantum
    number." The correct term is **principal quantum number**.
2.  **Group numbering:** standardize the primary convention while
    acknowledging common alternate A/B notation.
3.  **Configuration exceptions:** Pre-Reading/Case Study include
    exceptions, but the final 10 outcomes do not explicitly mention
    them. Clarify required mastery.
4.  **Core/valence:** explicit in Pre-Reading and recall, but not
    verbatim in the final 10 outcomes. Verify intended assessment
    status.
5.  **Shorthand/isoelectronic ions:** directly assessed on the Section
    Quiz but not explicit in the final 10 outcomes. Consider making them
    explicit if intended mastery targets.
6.  **Element-symbol memorization:** appears in the Periodic Table
    Pre-Reading but not the final 10 outcomes. Clarify expected
    memorization versus reference use.
7.  **Proofreading:** perform a later terminology/typographical cleanup
    across student-facing materials.
