## 1. Why This Section Exists

Section 2.1 is the bridge between the atomic/electronic model developed
in Unit 1 and the molecular-structure work that follows in Unit 2.

Students already have tools for reasoning about:

-   atomic identity;
-   ions and charge;
-   periodic-table position;
-   electron configurations;
-   valence electrons;
-   the octet rule.

Section 2.1 asks the next question:

> **What happens when atoms interact, and how does electron behavior
> determine the kind of compound that forms?**

A useful conceptual spine is:

``` text
periodic-table position + valence electrons
                 |
                 v
         electron behavior
          /             \
      transfer          sharing
         |                 |
         v                 v
   ionic bonding      covalent bonding
         |                 |
         v                 v
 ionic compounds     molecular compounds
         |                 |
 charge balance       electronegativity
         |                 |
 formula ratios       bond polarity
```

This section therefore should not be taught as two unrelated lists
called "ionic" and "covalent." The central organizing question is what
happens to valence electrons and what consequences follow.

## 7. Ionic Bonding Strand

### 7.1 Ion Formation

The review material begins by retrieving the Unit 1 relationship:

``` text
gain electrons → anion → negative charge
lose electrons → cation → positive charge
```

Students should reason about charge rather than memorize isolated signs.

### 7.2 Predicting Main-Group Ion Charge

The periodic table is used as a predictive tool:

-   Group 1 metals commonly lose one electron → 1+;
-   Group 2 metals commonly lose two electrons → 2+;
-   Group 17 nonmetals commonly gain one electron → 1−;
-   Group 16 nonmetals commonly gain two electrons → 2−.

The underlying explanatory idea is not simply "the group tells the
charge." Main-group atoms commonly gain or lose electrons to reach a
noble-gas-like electron count.

### 7.3 Isoelectronic Noble-Gas Relationships

Students are explicitly expected to connect ions to electron
configurations:

``` text
Ca → Ca2+ → 18 e− → isoelectronic with Ar
Br → Br−  → 36 e− → isoelectronic with Kr
```

This is a direct continuation of Section 1.2 electron-configuration
work.

### 7.4 Transition-Metal Caution

The review material explicitly notes that simple group-based charge
prediction is less reliable for transition metals and gives examples
such as Cu and Fe with multiple common charges.

The actual Section Quiz extends this idea by asking students to infer
variable metal charges from ionic formulas.

### 7.5 What an Ionic Bond Is

The collected materials describe ionic compounds in terms of:

-   metals tending to form cations;
-   nonmetals tending to form anions;
-   electron transfer during formation;
-   electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

A useful teaching distinction is:

``` text
electron transfer helps explain ion formation
                 ↓
electrostatic attraction holds the ions together
```

Students should not reduce "ionic bond" to the phrase "electrons are
transferred."

### 7.6 Ionic Structure

The NaCl review image represents an extended ionic arrangement in which
each ion interacts with surrounding ions.

This supports an important distinction:

> **Ionic compounds are not collections of discrete NaCl molecules.**

Their formulas represent charge-balanced **formula-unit ratios** in an
extended ionic structure.

## 8. Ionic Formulas and Charge Balance

The review materials state that ionic compounds are electrically neutral
overall and that formulas give the **simplest whole-number ratio** of
ions.

Examples include:

``` text
Na+  + Cl−  → NaCl

Ca2+ + 2 Cl− → CaCl2

2 Al3+ + 3 O2− → Al2O3

Mg2+ + S2− → MgS
```

The conceptual rule should come before any shortcut:

> **Total positive charge + total negative charge = 0.**

The "criss-cross" procedure appears in the review material, but it
should be treated as a shorthand derived from charge balance rather than
an unexplained algorithm.

Students should be able to verify every resulting formula by summing
charges.

## 9. Polyatomic Ions

### 9.1 Definition

The review material distinguishes:

-   **monatomic ion** --- one atom carrying a charge;
-   **polyatomic ion** --- a bonded group of atoms with an overall
    charge;
-   **oxyanion** --- a polyatomic ion containing oxygen.

### 9.2 Common Ions

The Section 2.1 materials expect familiarity with common ions including
examples such as:

-   ammonium;
-   acetate;
-   hydroxide;
-   cyanide;
-   carbonate;
-   nitrate;
-   phosphate;
-   sulfate;
-   chlorate;
-   chromate;
-   dichromate;
-   permanganate;
-   hydrogen carbonate;
-   hydrogen phosphate;
-   dihydrogen phosphate;
-   hydrogen sulfate.

The exact memorization expectations should be checked against any
reference materials permitted on assessments.

### 9.3 Parentheses

When more than one copy of a polyatomic ion is required, parentheses
preserve the ion as a unit:

``` text
Al3+ + SO4^2− → Al2(SO4)3
```

Students should understand why parentheses are needed rather than merely
copying formatting.

### 9.4 Oxyanion Naming Patterns

The review slides introduce relationships such as:

``` text
per-...-ate
...-ate
...-ite
hypo-...-ite
```

using chlorine oxyanions as examples.

This creates intentional overlap with the later Chemical Nomenclature
section. Section 2.1 introduces the ions because students need them for
ionic formulas; Section 2.4 will systematize naming more fully.

## 10. Covalent Bonding Strand

### 10.1 Shared Electrons

The review material defines a covalent bond in terms of electrons shared
between nuclei.

A useful contrast is:

``` text
ionic:    ions + electrostatic attraction
covalent: shared electron density between nuclei
```

### 10.2 Why a Covalent Bond Forms

The H2 potential-energy diagram provides a deeper model:

``` text
atoms far apart
      ↓ approach
attractions lower potential energy
      ↓
minimum potential energy
      ↓
equilibrium bond length
```

A bond therefore should not be explained only as "atoms want eight
electrons." The energy model gives students a physical reason that a
stable internuclear separation exists.

### 10.3 Octet Reasoning

The Pre-Reading quiz connects covalent sharing to the octet rule: shared
electrons can be counted with each bonded atom so that main-group atoms
often reach eight associated valence electrons (with hydrogen as the
familiar two-electron case).

This is useful preparation for Lewis structures in Section 2.2.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 11. Electronegativity and Bond Polarity

### 11.1 Electronegativity

The collected materials define electronegativity as an atom's tendency
to attract bonding electron density toward itself.

The periodic trend emphasized is:

``` text
across a period → electronegativity generally increases
down a group    → electronegativity generally decreases
```

The highest values therefore lie toward the upper-right region of the
periodic table.

### 11.2 Equal Versus Unequal Sharing

``` text
similar electronegativities
        ↓
more even sharing
        ↓
nonpolar / less polar covalent bond

different electronegativities
        ↓
uneven sharing
        ↓
polar covalent bond
```

### 11.3 Partial Charges

For a polar covalent bond:

-   the more electronegative atom carries partial negative character,
    δ−;
-   the less electronegative atom carries partial positive character,
    δ+.

These are **partial charges**, not full ionic charges.

### 11.4 Bond Character as a Continuum

The review material explicitly shows increasing electronegativity
difference producing increasing bond polarity and increasing ionic
character.

Students should therefore avoid treating bond type as if nature has
perfectly sharp boundaries.

### 11.5 Ranking Bond Polarity

Students are expected not only to label bonds but also to compare them.

The review and Section Quiz ask students to determine which bond is:

-   more polar;
-   less polar;
-   least polar but still polar covalent;
-   most polar without being treated as ionic.

This requires comparing electronegativity differences.

## 12. Ionic Versus Covalent Compounds

The Critical Chemistry integration lesson explicitly requires students
to compare both bonding and physical properties.

A useful comparison frame is:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Feature                 Ionic                   Molecular/Covalent
  ----------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------
  Typical elemental       metal + nonmetal / ions nonmetal + nonmetal
  combination                                     

  Electron picture        ion formation;          shared electron density
                          electrostatic           
                          attraction              

  Smallest written        formula unit / ratio    molecular formula
  representation                                  

  Typical melting         generally higher        generally lower
  behavior                                        

  Mechanical character    often hard/brittle      often softer

  State near room         commonly solids         may be solid, liquid,
  conditions                                      or gas
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

These are **general patterns**, not definitions of bond type.

High-value class time should therefore emphasize **connections and

### High-value instructor moves

-   reconnect bonding to Unit 1 electron configurations and periodicity;
-   distinguish electron transfer during ion formation from
    electrostatic attraction in an ionic solid;
-   make charge neutrality the reason behind ionic formulas;
-   prevent "criss-cross" from becoming an unexamined trick;

-   distinguish formula units from molecules;
-   use the H2 potential-energy curve to explain why covalent bonds

-   distinguish full ionic charge from partial δ+/δ− charge;
-   make bond polarity a continuum rather than a rigid vocabulary
    exercise;
-   compare macroscopic properties with submicroscopic bonding models;
-   require students to predict before showing the answer.

## 20. Common Confusion Candidates

> **Status: INFERRED from the collected instructional and assessment
> materials**

Likely high-value misconceptions include:

1.  **"Ionic bond = electron transfer."**\
    Electron transfer forms ions; electrostatic attraction between
    oppositely charged ions is the bonding interaction.

2.  **"Ionic compounds are molecules."**\
    Their formulas represent simplest whole-number ion ratios in an
    extended ionic structure.

3.  **"Criss-cross is the rule."**\
    Charge neutrality is the rule; criss-cross is shorthand.

4.  **Failing to reduce ratios.**\
    Mg2+ and S2− produce MgS, not Mg2S2.

5.  **Losing the identity of a polyatomic ion.**\
    Parentheses are required when multiple copies of the ion appear.

6.  **Confusing ionic charge with partial charge.**\
    `Cl−` and `δ−` mean different things.

7.  **Thinking covalent means perfectly equal sharing.**\
    Polar covalent bonds involve unequal sharing.

8.  **Memorizing electronegativity without periodic reasoning.**

9.  **Treating ionic/covalent as an absolute binary boundary.**\
    The review material explicitly introduces increasing ionic character
    with increasing electronegativity difference.

10. **Using physical properties as definitions.**\
    Melting point or hardness supports classification but does not
    define the underlying bond.

11. **Applying main-group charge patterns uncritically to transition
    metals.**

## 21. Chemistry Triplet Opportunities

The Consolidation instructions encourage macroscopic, submicroscopic,
and symbolic representations.

Section 2.1 is especially well suited to this.

### Ionic example --- NaCl

**Macroscopic:** white crystalline solid with properties unlike
elemental Na or Cl2.

**Submicroscopic:** extended arrangement of Na+ and Cl− ions attracted
electrostatically.

**Symbolic:** `NaCl`, representing a 1:1 formula-unit ratio.

### Covalent example --- HCl

**Macroscopic:** molecular substance with properties distinct from an
ionic salt.

**Submicroscopic:** shared electron density shifted toward Cl.

**Symbolic:** `Hδ+—Clδ−`.

Moving among these three representations can help prevent students from
treating formulas as arbitrary strings of symbols.
